Could a program undo the drop in NAEP scores?

Between 2019 and 2024, scores on the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) fell in reading and math, and the lowest-scoring students fell the furthest. Use this tool to design a program, such as tutoring or summer school, and see how much of that drop it would undo for students at different score levels.

What you are looking at

NAEP

NAEP, often called the Nation's Report Card, tests a nationally representative sample of students in grades 4, 8, and 12. It gives the same test the same way every time, so scores can be compared across years.

Different students each time

The students tested in 2024 are not the ones tested in 2019. The tool compares 2024's fourth graders with 2019's fourth graders, and the same for grades 8 and 12. It does not measure how much any one student lost.

Percentiles

Line up every student from lowest score to highest. A student at the 10th percentile scored above 10 percent of students; the 50th percentile is the middle; the 90th is near the top. NAEP reports scores at the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles.

The widening gap

In every subject and grade here, scores fell more at the 10th percentile than at the 90th, so low and high scorers ended up further apart.

Standard deviations

Researchers usually report a program's effect in standard deviations (SD), a unit that lets different tests be compared. The tool uses SD for both the drop and the program.

How to use it

Pick a subject and grade, then set up the program on the left. The chart shows how far each percentile sits below its 2019 score, with and without the program. Zero means back to 2019.

How the tool works

NAEP publishes scores at five percentiles. The tool draws a smooth curve through those points to estimate the scores in between. That is why the charts run from the 10th to the 90th percentile and no further.

Changing who takes part does not change how many take part. The tool moves the same number of seats between low and high scorers. No group can go above 100 percent; any extra seats go to groups that still have room. The boost works the same way, so its average across all students stays at the value you set.

The two ways of counting give different answers when only some students at a score level take part. "All students" lines everyone up again after the program. Students who took part can move past classmates who did not, so a program that reaches only some students spreads scores out. "Same students" follows each starting group and averages its gain. The two agree when everyone at a score level takes part, or no one does.

The tool assumes that, among students with the same score, those who take part would gain as much as those who do not. A share above 100 percent means a percentile ends up above its 2019 score.

Technical notes

The dashed curve is D(p)/S, the 2024 quantile minus the 2019 quantile at percentile p, divided by the 2019 national standard deviation S for that subject and grade. The restoration requirement g*(p) = −D(p)/S is the same curve with the sign flipped. Quantile functions are rebuilt from the published percentiles by monotone (Fritsch–Carlson) interpolation with normal tails. Participation and the treated effect are linear in prior rank with mean held fixed; participation is capped at 1 and water-filled to conserve the seat budget.

"All students" is the distributional estimand: post-program quantiles of the exact treated/untreated mixture, calibrated to reproduce the published knots. "Same students" is the group estimand, Q(p) + π(p)δ(p). The R pipeline in analysis/ is the reference implementation; analysis/tests/test-tool-engine.mjs checks this page against it.
